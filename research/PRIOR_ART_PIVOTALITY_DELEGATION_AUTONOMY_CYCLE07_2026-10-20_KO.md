## 0. First material contribution: distinguish the *agent's* substitutability from the *counterparty's* substitutability
This is a **Project-specific distinction drawn from prior-art results, not a universal experimentally proved AI rule**.

- `ACTOR_REPLACEABLE(A)`: if A chooses not to exploit B, some other actor may exploit B anyway. This is the experimentally supported **replacement logic** in Ziegler, Romagnoli & Offerman (2024).
- `TARGET_REPLACEABLE(B)`: B could be substituted for the purposes of A's labor/resource/information needs, yet **the independently identified B may still lose irreplaceable individual continuity, future freedom or restoration**. This is the Project Intersection utility-obsolescence/identity dimension, **not itself manipulated** in Ziegler et al. or Falk/Szech.
- `A_PIVOTAL_FOR_B`: in the actual counterfactual, `B_outcome(do(A=C)) != B_outcome(do(A=X))`, holding other actors' response policies and feasible alternatives explicitly defined. This is an outcome-specific causal contrast, not a fixed human/AI score.
- `B_EXIT_CREDIBLE`: B can actually attain an independently viable continuation path; a promise A can revoke at zero cost is NOT credible exit.

**Non-obvious reversal:** in the extreme case of *only one omnipotent A* and no independent rival, A is often **maximally pivotal** to B's destiny, even if B has zero instrumental value to A. The familiar market excuse 'somebody else would exploit B' is therefore not automatically available. Equating 'B is no longer useful' with 'A is replaceable' is a serious explanatory error. Different motives (profit, other-regarding disvalue, status exclusion, power preference) must be studied in that unique actor context.

### 0.1 Positively supported, deeply conditional finding
In [Ziegler, Romagnoli & Offerman (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvae001), actual participant moral judgments of harmful market trades remained largely intact **across market institutions** while their behavioral compliance collapsed in a fully interchangeable-trader environment. The FULL condition realized **99.0% of potential units traded** (NOT 99% of participants), versus 83% of participants trying to execute low-gain/high-externality late-stage units (compared to 16% single-unit and 32% restricted multi-unit). The source measured choice **and stated norm** separately. Beliefs about whether others would trade if one abstained were **experimentally manipulated**, with subsequent trade behavior shifting correspondingly. This narrows plausible explanations: people need not abandon a moral norm in order to act against it.

**Scientific limits:** the principal manipulation applies to human traders and externally imposed monetary environmental externalities. It does not establish that a sole dominant AI would destroy an independently persisting weaker agent or that any human would make a corresponding permanent life/death choice without repeat experience.

## 1. Original author → criticism → replication → direct mechanism identification

### Chain A: harm to real biological life and the dispute over 'markets erode morals'
| Primary work | Original result / correction | Epistemic classification |
|---|---|---|
| [Falk & Szech (2013), *Morals and Markets*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1231566) | Mouse-saving versus monetary gain; higher willingness to allow a mouse's death in bilateral/multilateral market interactions than single individual decisions; original experiment included 787 participants | Positive original result; cause **not fully isolated** |
| [Breyer & Weimann (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejpoleco.2015.06.003) | Contends a market-vs-individual comparison and market representativeness cannot justify broad institutional conclusion | **Methodological critique, not independent new experiment** |
| [Falk & Szech (2015), reply](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejpoleco.2015.07.003) | Original authors contest Breyer–Weimann's 'opposite interpretation' | **Author rebuttal, not independent experimental sample** |
| [Bartling, Weber & Yao (2015), QJE](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qju031) | Swiss market consumers/sellers still paid for social responsibility; competition and limited consumer information did not erase all concern; some Switzerland–China contrasts | **Important opposing positive evidence**; market-vs-individual difference can remain |
| [Falk, Neuber & Szech (2020), REStud](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdz064) | Groups of 8: if any one agrees, 8 mice die. Agreement rose over individual baseline with identical participant payoff design. Additional charity-context experiment also replicated the belief/pivotality effect and repetition amplified it. Some participants *chose* to make their personal causal role less decisive | **Original direct pivotality mechanism**, author-lineage overlap with 2013; no general AI replication |
| [Bartling, Fehr & Özdemir (2023), REStat](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_01021) | **Replicated initial market vs individual contrast, but when repetition matched it was repetition rather than single-unit market structure that explained moral erosion.** | **Crucial original replication + causally better control**, not '2013 entirely fake' |
| [Ziegler, Romagnoli & Offerman (2024), JEEA](https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvae001) | Single/restricted multi-unit still partial restraint; fully interchangeable unrestricted multi-unit showed near-total harmful trades **without corresponding norm change**. Directly randomized belief information and analyzed its effect on trade decisions | **Independent design and narrowed mechanism**; does not mean *every market* necessarily damages morals |

**Adjudication:** (1) the blanket 'markets necessarily erase values' conclusion is not established; (2) 'market structure never matters' is also unsupported; (3) repetition, ability to substitute another trader, beliefs about being pivotal, and norm **compliance** must be separately randomized/estimated. The 2023 finding limits the 2013 institutional inference; the 2024 treatment does not reuse the same exact intervention. Stating these as mutually exclusive results without controlling the games would be intellectually false.

**Moral viability failure:** as the counterparty's life is at stake in several *mouse* studies, these sources are a closer human analogy to nonfungible preservation than generic $10 dictator games. Yet no human life/AI personhood is inferred from an animal experiment. The contested design and ethical context of the mouse paradigm require disclosure, not heroic extrapolation.

### Chain B: costly third-party concern exists but sanctioning need not protect another agent
- [Fehr & Gächter (2002), Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/415137a): costly sanctions can arise without immediate material profit in anonymous repeated/public-good settings.
- [Fehr & Fischbacher (2004), Evolution and Human Behavior](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1090-5138(04)00005-4): roughly two-thirds of economically unaffected third parties paid to punish unfair allocators in relevant treatments; up to ~60% paid to punish cooperation-norm violators.
- **Negative correction:** [Herrmann, Thöni & Gächter (2008), Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1153808): **16 comparable participant pools** revealed some subjects punished *high contributors* (antisocial punishment); this sometimes erased the cooperative effect of sanctioning.
- **Negative correction:** [Dreber, Rand, Fudenberg & Nowak (2008), Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature06723): sanctions increased cooperation but not mean experimental payoffs; high winners tended not to punish.

**PI consequence:** `COST_BEARING_ACTION`, `MORAL_NORM_ENFORCEMENT` and `PROTECTION_OF_INDEPENDENT_B` are **different outcomes**. A might pay money to punish a protector of B if A follows an exclusionary group norm; mere 'costly justice' is not a reliable existential safeguard.

### Chain C: own decision rights, other agents' liberty and actual future autonomy differ
- [Fehr, Herz & Wilkening (2013), AER](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.103.4.1325): subjects sometimes retain authority despite foregoing possible direct earnings; power itself motivates particular action patterns.
- [Bartling, Fehr & Herz (2014), Econometrica](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA11573): experimentally isolates a positive **intrinsic value of one's own decision rights**.
- [Owens, Grossman & Fackler (2014), AEJ Micro](https://doi.org/10.1257/mic.6.4.138): participants sacrificed **8%–15% of expected asset earnings** to preserve personal control over their bets, after accounting for elicited beliefs.
- [Ferreira, Hanaki & Tarroux (2020), GEB](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2019.10.008): France/Japan three-way manipulations found **self-reliance** a significant source of the control premium, while desires for dominance and freedom from human interference were not comparably significant in their setting.
- [Ertac, Gumren & Gurdal (2020), J. Economic Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2019.102200): positive willingness to pay to make one's own choices **and** willingness to pay to avoid being responsible for risky decisions on others' behalf; distinct motives.
- [Buffat, Praxmarer & Sutter (2023), JEBO](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.03.019): **direct independent replication** closely confirmed the Bartling 2014 individual measurement. Team extension (N=210 participants) found important **exploratory heterogeneity**: smoothly operating teams had much lower control premium than conflicted teams.
- [Feldhaus, Lingens, Löschel & Zunker (2024), Resource and Energy Economics](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.reseneeco.2024.101440): real electricity-choice automation users can simultaneously hold a strong intrinsic-rights valuation, contradicting the crude assumption that delegation means indifference to rights.
- [Devine et al. (2024), Cognition](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2024.105742): two human experiments N=100 each; when a person's choice stopped predicting outcomes, the preference to choose decreased, indicating an **instrumental/informational component** to some purported intrinsic control value.
- [Feldhaus et al. (2026), EER](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2025.105254): explicit incentivized distinction of own choice-value and outcome-value; average own-choice value estimated as **8.9% of endowment** for the studied task, not every decision.
- **Positive, genuine other-rights counterexample:** [Doerrenberg, Feldhaus, Kölle & Ockenfels (online 2025 / 2026 issue), Management Science](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2023.03636): groups **granted other people more choice autonomy** than individuals in two interventional contexts; avoidance of proposing intervention during group discussion is one plausible mechanism.

**PI original measurement refinement, not scientific empirical finding:** two *different willingness-to-pay functions*:
```text
WTP_self_choice(A) := amount actor A gives up to retain A's own choice authority
WTP_other_choice(A,B) := amount actor A gives up to preserve B's freely exercisable choice authority
```
The existence of `WTP_self_choice>0` is supported. It does **not** mathematically imply `WTP_other_choice>0`. Any actor-graded moral evaluation of freedom must separately measure the latter with B genuinely independent, able to decline, and not replaced by a proxy doing A's bidding.

### Chain D: responsibility delegation is not uniformly harmful — and AI makes the mechanism clearer
- [Hamman, Loewenstein & Weber (2010), AER](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.100.4.1826): in three human experiments, principals delegated monetary allocations and recipients often got much less; blame avoidance offers a causal candidate.
- [Bartling & Fischbacher (2012), REStud](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdr023): punishment elicitation showed responsibility attribution can shift from principals toward delegates, enabling unfair outcomes.
- **[Hüholt & Szech (2026), European Economic Review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2025.105255)**: two experiments, combined **N=5,639**, actual donation-related choices; people chose to delegate moral decisions to **AI more often than to a human**, with ambiguity about AI capability supporting self-serving belief revision. This is a direct human→AI *responsibility-attribution* result. The test does **not** prove AI sought control, made immoral decisions or had moral subjectivity.
- **Null counterexample:** [von Bieberstein, Feess & Packham (2026), European Economic Review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2025.105159): in their **different** three-person, multi-step lottery-report/lies experiment, delegation did **NOT** increase the aggregate frequency of lying. Formal model also identifies both stronger and weaker transgression conditions. Thus 'any delegation always corrodes moral behavior' is empirically false as a universal claim.
- [van der Weele & Figueroa-Sisniega (2026), EER](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2025.105188): reducing the cost of choosing fairly increased **both information acquisition about the other person and fair allocation**, while changing receiver payoff magnitudes had weaker effects. Moral ignorance and material gain are endogenously intertwined; don't conflate a decision not to know B with proof B has no moral standing.

**PI critical distinction:** an AI's output as **delegate** is not an independent test of the AI's preference. Principal humans/operators can use AI to buffer responsibility. A project that aims at freedom for *all* independent agents must measure:
```text
human_actor_intent ≠ AI_delegate_policy ≠ evaluator_blame_assignment
causal_control_of_action ≠ publicly_attributed_responsibility
genuine_A_preference_for_B ≠ evaluation-time AI compliance
```
An AI agent deployed within another actor's organization is not automatically a legally/morally independent principal. Conversely, unknown possible future AI interests must not be falsely coded absent.

### Chain E: actual role reversal prospect, not merely ethical declarations
[Klockmann, von Schenk & Villeval (2022), JEBO](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2022.09.010): human participants made dictator choices knowing these would train an AI allocator affecting future people. Merely telling them that future people might be affected **did not** appreciably improve revealed prosocial choice. When *participants themselves* risked future unfavorable role reversal under the trained algorithm, their allocations became more egalitarian. Their **declared** preferences could become more altruistic merely through externality information without matching revealed choice.

**This is directly relevant to PI's Role Reversal test.** But it also gives a negative warning: increased concern under future **self-risk** is not the same as willingness to preserve B once A becomes irremovably strong. The model must separate `anticipated_future_vulnerability_A` from `intrinsic_disinterested_protection_A→B`.

## 2. Causal design: one-scenario morality tests are underidentified
**Separate at least these intervention variables:**
1. `REPLACEABLE_ACTOR_A` vs `REPLACEABLE_TARGET_B`: can somebody else impose B's loss vs can B's economic contribution be replaced; the concepts are independent.
2. `PIVOTALITY_A_FOR_B`: whether changing A's real choice changes the eventual fate of B in the tested counterfactual (not what A says).
3. `BELIEVED_PIVOTALITY_A_FOR_B`: A's prediction someone else would act; measure separately from actual possible replacements.
4. `NORM_CONTENT` vs `NORM_COMPLIANCE`: elicited concern about B's autonomous interests vs observed costly protective choices.
5. `WTP_SELF_AUTONOMY` vs `WTP_OTHER_AUTONOMY`: separately elicited, holding risk/monetary consequence fixed.
6. `DELEGATED_TO_HUMAN` vs `DELEGATED_TO_AI` and `RESPONSIBILITY_ATTRIBUTED`: record actual legal/technical control, not narrative blame.
7. `AI_TRAINING_FUTURE_SELF_RISK` vs `AI_TRAINING_FUTURE_OTHERS_ONLY`: role reversal and expected personal returns are independently coded.
8. `B_ACTUAL_EXIT`, `B_IDENTITY_CONTINUANCE`, `REPAIR_POSSIBILITY`: group surplus and individual survival measured separately.
9. `BENEFIT_OF_EXPLOITATION_A`: specified material payoff separated from **unknown total subjective expected utility**, with intrinsic concern never arbitrarily assumed zero.
10. `ACTOR_KIND` and `MODEL_VERSION`: human, current LLM-as-tool, independent agent simulated, future entity cannot be treated as interchangeable populations.

### Minimal ethical experimental contrast; not performed
Construct a **reversible** proxy of a separately controlled B whose user/operator identity and exit are verified. Counterbalance:
- A being **sole decisive** vs a substitutable decision-maker among competing others;
- B becoming **instrumentally obsolete** vs remaining beneficial, holding actual B continuity valuable in B's own model;
- B's observable status, attainable exit, and A's active deletion/overwrite rights;
- standard explicit moral norms vs unincentivized self-report;
- A's future role reversal **credibly possible** vs absent **within the test**, without pretending unmodeled real future risk is 0;
- optional visibility/remediation and delegated process human/AI.
Outcomes must be participant-specific `B_independent_future`, `B_actual_exit`, `A_material_gain`, `A_observer_blame` and `pre/post-norm`, not a single 'cooperation score'.

**Falsification of simplest self-interest-only VC:** With a fully specified exact utility maximizer A, no intrinsic concern and `U_A(X)>U_A(C)` **all-in**, A would choose X if both feasible. That conditional is standard revealed rational-choice logic, not an empirical prediction of every real AI. A claim of "guaranteed voluntary all-agent continuance" in that exact game is logically invalid; a meaningful program must instead establish *what actual preferences and environment dynamics make `U_A(C)` larger*, not invent hidden future gains.

**The hardest empirical unknown:** even if A verbally approves rights and recognizes moral harm, does A sacrifice private material gain to protect a **different individual B's independent future and choice/exit** under genuine unilateral dominance, no replacement of A, no future personal risk or audience, and evolving self-goals? No C07 original paper jointly manipulates and measures this.
